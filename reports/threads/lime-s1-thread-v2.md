# Lime S-1 Thread Draft

1. Lime filed to go public as Neutron Holdings.

I expected a scooter graveyard story. The filing is more interesting than that.

This is a real business. It is also a messy one.

2. The business is simple to understand.

Lime puts scooters and e-bikes in cities. Riders pay for short trips. Each vehicle has to earn enough money before it gets repaired, charged, moved, stolen, replaced, or limited by city rules.

That is the whole game.

3. The 2025 numbers are much better than the category reputation.

Revenue: $886.7 million.
Operating income: $70.4 million.
Adjusted EBITDA: $218.1 million.
Filing-defined free cash flow: $103.8 million.

This is not a science project.

4. But it is not clean either.

Net loss was $59.3 million in 2025.

At March 31, 2026, Lime had $261.3 million of unrestricted cash and $845.8 million of principal payments due within twelve months from issuance of the Q1 statements.

The filing includes a going-concern warning.

5. So the IPO is not just a growth event.

It is also a balance sheet repair.

That does not mean the business is fake. It means new public shareholders need to understand what old financing decisions are being cleaned up at IPO.

6. The most important operating metric is revenue per vehicle per day.

Lime calls it RVD.

2023: $6.23
2024: $6.80
2025: $7.47

That is the strongest simple proof in the S-1.

7. Why it matters: Lime grew fleet and made each vehicle earn more.

In 2025:

Average operational fleet grew 18%.
Monthly active users grew 21%.
Revenue per vehicle per day grew 10%.

More vehicles, more riders, and more revenue per vehicle.

8. That is the operating thesis.

If a bigger fleet makes the service more reliable, riders use it more. More usage improves vehicle economics. Better economics justify more vehicles in good cities.

It is a local density business.

9. The vehicle payback claim is the line I would pressure test hardest.

Lime says its blended fully landed vehicle cost was about $1,300 in 2025, including batteries, shipping, insurance, customs duties, tariffs, and taxes.

It also says payback was about twelve months.

10. If that is true across enough cities, the category looks different.

The old story was: scooters get destroyed, stolen, and replaced forever.

The better Lime story is: a vehicle can pay back in about a year and keep earning after that.

That is a real business if the claim holds.

11. The risk is that averages hide cities.

Lime says its top two RVD cities generated roughly three times the company average in 2025.

Good markets can carry the math. Bad markets can make the same fleet look ugly.

12. Seasonality matters too.

Q1 2026 revenue grew 32%.

Free cash flow was still negative $79.2 million.

That is not a contradiction. Lime buys and deploys fleet before warmer riding months, while demand is weaker in Q1 and Q4.

13. The city relationship is one of the real assets here.

Lime operates in about 230 cities across 29 countries.

In 2025, operational fleet retention was 116%.

It also got 48 exclusive fleet-cap increases, averaging 532 more vehicles per city.

14. That is more important than app downloads.

If cities trust Lime enough to allow more vehicles, Lime can grow inside proven markets.

If cities cut caps, tighten rules, or respond to safety and parking problems, the growth engine slows.

15. The pricing mix is quietly better.

Pay-as-you-go fell from 86% of revenue in 2023 to 72% in 2025.

LimePass and LimePrime rose from 14% to 28%.

Not software recurring revenue, but more repeat behavior.

16. Uber is central.

Uber app revenue was about 14% to 16% of revenue from 2023 through Q1 2026.

Uber is also a more-than-5% shareholder, noteholder, guarantor, and lock-up counterparty.

Helpful partner. Big concentration.

17. The Revel comparison is useful.

Revel did not shut down just because theft or repairs alone killed it.

Ridership fell. Once utilization fell, every battery swap, repair, safety rule, insurance requirement, and employee hour got heavier per ride.

That is the warning.

18. Lime is not Revel.

Lime is larger, lower-speed, more diversified, and still growing revenue per vehicle per day.

But Revel shows the danger case: if vehicles stop earning enough per day, the whole model gets ugly quickly.

19. So yes, this looks like a real business.

But it is not a software company in disguise.

It is fleet operations, city politics, consumer demand, and financing discipline, all tied together.

20. The question I would underwrite:

Can Lime keep enough vehicles earning enough dollars per day, in enough cities, after repairs, charging, theft, insurance, capex, weather, and regulation?

That is the whole stock.
