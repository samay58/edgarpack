# Lime S-1 Thread Highlights

Saved from the Lime / Neutron Holdings S-1 discussion on May 11, 2026.

## Simple Business Read

Lime is a real business, but it is not a clean software business. It is a dense local operations business that happens to have an app. The app helps demand, pricing, routing, and payments, but the real business is putting vehicles in the right places, keeping them working, getting cities to allow more of them, and earning enough per vehicle before repairs, charging, insurance, theft, and replacement eat the margin.

The best simple model is this: every scooter or bike is a small earning machine. The question is how many dollars it can earn per day before it has to be repaired, moved, charged, stolen, retired, or blocked by city rules.

## What Looked Real

Lime has reached scale. Revenue was $886.7 million in 2025. Operating income was $70.4 million. Adjusted EBITDA was $218.1 million. Filing-defined free cash flow was $103.8 million.

The operating metrics moved together in the right direction. In 2025, average operational fleet grew 18%, monthly active users grew 21%, and revenue per vehicle per day grew 10% to $7.47. That matters because the company did not grow only by adding more vehicles. Each vehicle also earned more.

The vehicle payback claim is the center of the story. Lime says its 2025 blended fully landed vehicle cost was about $1,300 and payback was about twelve months. If that holds across enough cities, the category looks much more investable than the old scooter graveyard narrative.

City permission is a real advantage. Lime operates in about 230 cities across 29 countries. It disclosed 116% operational fleet retention and 48 exclusive fleet-cap increases in 2025, with an average increase of 532 vehicles. Cities are not just a regulatory risk. They are also the growth gate.

The pricing mix is improving. Pay-as-you-go revenue fell from 86% of revenue in 2023 to 72% in 2025. LimePass and LimePrime rose from 14% to 28%. This is not software-style recurring revenue, but it does show more repeat rider behavior.

## What Still Makes It Hard

The IPO is partly a balance sheet repair. At March 31, 2026, Lime had $261.3 million of unrestricted cash and $845.8 million of principal payments due within twelve months from issuance of the Q1 statements. The filing includes a going-concern warning unless the IPO or alternative financing happens.

Free cash flow needs careful reading. The filing-defined 2025 free cash flow number is positive, but supplier payment terms helped capex timing. EdgarPack's computed free cash flow was wrong in this run because capex was mis-bound across annual and interim tables. The filing reconciliation was the source of truth.

Seasonality is not a footnote. Q1 2026 revenue grew 32%, but free cash flow was negative $79.2 million. The company buys and deploys fleet ahead of the warmer riding season, while demand is lower in Q1 and Q4.

Uber is not just a partner. Uber is a distribution channel, more-than-5% shareholder, noteholder, guarantor, and lock-up counterparty. That can help Lime, but it is also a concentration risk.

## Revel Comparison

Revel did not shut down simply because repairs or theft alone killed it. The official reason was falling ridership and a pivot to EV ride-hail and charging. The deeper read is that lower utilization made a field-ops-heavy, safety-sensitive, city-regulated moped fleet too hard to justify.

Repairs, theft, battery swaps, labor, insurance, and city rules were real pressures. They became fatal because utilization fell. That is the lesson for Lime.

Lime is not Revel. Lime is larger, more diversified, lower-speed, and still growing revenue per vehicle per day. But Revel is the warning case: if rides per vehicle fall, every fixed or semi-fixed cost gets heavier per ride.

## The One Question

The business comes down to utilization, but utilization is not the whole business. It is the scorecard.

If Lime can keep revenue per vehicle per day high while expanding fleet caps in good cities, the model has real legs. If utilization slips, city relationships worsen, or maintenance and theft costs rise faster than revenue per vehicle, the economics can break quickly.

The underwriting question is simple:

Can Lime keep enough vehicles earning enough dollars per day, in enough cities, after repairs, charging, theft, insurance, capex, weather, and regulation?

## Diligence Questions To Keep

- How much does twelve-month vehicle payback vary by city?
- What percentage of vehicles reach the assumed useful life?
- How much of RVD growth is utilization versus pricing?
- How much of 2025 free cash flow came from payment-term timing?
- What happens to cash needs in a weak Q1 or weak summer?
- What are the real theft, vandalism, and maintenance trends by market?
- How much control does Uber have over Lime's demand, financing, and post-IPO stock overhang?
- How much dilution comes from old notes converting at IPO?
