# Revel Shared Moped Shutdown Research

Captured: 2026-05-11

Question: Did Revel shut down because of repairs, theft, capex, and city rules? Short answer: partly, but not as the official headline reason. Revel said the final shutdown was driven by falling ridership and a strategic pivot to EV ride-hail and charging. The deeper operating read is that declining ridership made a field-ops-heavy, safety-sensitive, city-regulated moped fleet uneconomic.

## The Final Shutdown

Revel ended shared mopeds in New York City and San Francisco, its last two moped markets, with November 18, 2023 as the last day of service. TechCrunch reported from a company-wide email by CEO Frank Reig that the service was strained and ridership was no longer what it had been. A Revel spokesperson said ridership fell about 30% year over year in both New York and San Francisco from peak summer rides, making the service unsustainable. The company laid off about 67 moped-operations employees and said it would focus on EV ride-hail and public fast charging. Source: TechCrunch, November 3, 2023.

So the official answer was not "theft killed it" or "repairs killed it." The official answer was lower utilization plus a better use of management/capital elsewhere.

## Earlier Market Exits

Austin ended earlier. TechCrunch reported Revel exited Austin in December 2020 after CEO Frank Reig cited Austin's less dense urban core, car culture, and COVID's effect on travel. That matters because it points to the same core issue: mopeds need dense, repeat, urban demand.

Washington, D.C. ended in November 2022. DCist reported that Revel described it as a business decision while focusing on electric rideshare and public fast charging.

Miami had also been exited by the time Revel shut down the final NYC/SF markets. I did not find a clean Miami-specific company explanation beyond the broader business-focus rationale.

## Safety And City Rules

NYC was the main regulatory story. Revel suspended NYC service on July 28, 2020 after fatal crashes. NYC DOT later allowed a relaunch only with stricter safety protocols: mandatory rider training, helmet selfie before rides, stronger account controls, GPS enforcement, public reporting, data sharing with DOT, and no rides from midnight to 5 a.m. for the first 60 days.

NYC DOT said Revel grew from 1,000 to 3,000 mopeds and had 330 injury crashes in the first seven months of 2020. Later, Local Law 67 made it unlawful to operate a moped-share system without DOT authorization and required rules around safety, maintenance, rider accountability, helmet evidence, outreach, equity, parking, insurance, data, and records.

This did not by itself cause the 2023 shutdown, but it changed the business. A moped-share company was not just renting vehicles. It was operating a regulated transport system with real safety exposure.

## Maintenance, Batteries, Theft, And Labor

The operating cost story is real.

Reported facts:

- Revel used employees for field operations, not only gig workers.
- Moped operations had about 67 staffers affected in the 2023 shutdown.
- Employees swapped batteries in the field.
- Mopeds had about a 50-mile range.
- Revel removed mopeds for routine maintenance every 4-6 months and modeled about a 3-year asset life.
- Revel suspended Bronx service in December 2021 after battery thefts made mopeds unusable. Bronx Times reported batteries were a target.
- amNewYork reported that by the end, the moped service had become more intensive to maintain while fewer riders were using it. Workers had to keep swapping batteries, charging mopeds, and doing more costly maintenance as the mopeds aged.

The key point is the interaction. High maintenance is survivable with high utilization. Lower utilization makes every battery swap, repair, insurance requirement, safety protocol, and employee hour heavier per ride.

## The Best Economic Read

THE CITY reported Revel had 137,000 NYC rides in July 2023, down 30% year over year, with up to 3,500 vehicles on the street. If you divide those figures naively, that is roughly 1.3 rides per vehicle per day in a peak summer month. That is an estimate, because "up to 3,500 vehicles" may not equal average active fleet. But it gives the shape of the problem.

An owned moped fleet with employee labor, battery swaps, maintenance, insurance, city compliance, safety training, and theft losses cannot work if peak-season utilization is weak.

## What This Means For Lime

Revel is a warning for Lime, but not a perfect comparison.

What transfers:

- Utilization is everything. If rides per vehicle fall, fleet economics get ugly quickly.
- City trust matters. Permits, safety incidents, parking, data sharing, and local politics are not side issues.
- Repairs, theft, battery work, and field labor are real cost centers.
- A micromobility company can look visible and popular in a city while still not being economically healthy.

What does not transfer cleanly:

- Revel's product was heavier and faster. Mopeds were closer to motor vehicles, required helmets, could carry passengers, and faced more severe safety scrutiny.
- Lime has a much larger footprint: about 230 cities and 29 countries in 2025.
- Lime is still growing: 2025 revenue was $886.7 million, up 29%, and average operational fleet, MAU, and revenue per vehicle per day all grew.
- Lime disclosed about $1,300 fully landed vehicle cost and roughly twelve-month payback for its blended e-scooter/e-bike fleet. I did not find similar public unit economics for Revel.

Investor read: Revel did not prove Lime is fake. It proved the danger case. If utilization softens, city relationships worsen, or vehicle maintenance/theft/safety costs rise, the economics can break fast. Lime's S-1 is stronger than Revel's end-state because it shows growth, scale, and free cash flow. But the Revel story is exactly why Lime's vehicle payback, fleet retention, city permits, and insurance/safety disclosures matter.
